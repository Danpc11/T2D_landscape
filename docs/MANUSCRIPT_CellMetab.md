# Insulin resistance fragments a coordinated, clock-coupled transcriptional response in humans

## Highlights

- The resting muscle transcriptome of 49 clamped individuals carries no information about insulin sensitivity (0 genes at FDR < 0.1)
- Healthy muscle responds to insulin along a single direction shared by individuals, assembled over 4 h, that resets the peripheral clock output (DBP, PER2, NR1D2)
- Insulin-resistant and diabetic muscle respond with the same magnitude but each individual in a different direction, and the clock no longer responds
- Bariatric surgery restores the magnitude of the adipose response but not its coordination; the organs of one person share one transcriptional state that drifts continuously, without a second basin

## eTOC blurb

## Summary

Type 2 diabetes is defined by what insulin fails to do, yet most molecular studies profile tissues at rest. Reanalysing 23 public human transcriptomic cohorts, including 123 individuals with biopsies before and during hyperinsulinaemic clamps, 253 GTEx donors with paired organs and 49 clamped individuals with resting biopsies, we find that the resting muscle transcriptome contains no information about insulin sensitivity, whereas the response to insulin does. In healthy muscle the response is coordinated: individuals move along one shared transcriptional direction that takes hours to assemble and includes a resetting of the peripheral clock output (DBP, PER2, NR1D2). In insulin-resistant and diabetic muscle the response has the same magnitude but lacks a shared direction, and the clock no longer responds. Interventions that restore insulin sensitivity or response magnitude do not detectably restore coordination. The organs of one person share one transcriptional state that drifts continuously with disease, without a second basin. Insulin resistance is a loss of coordination of the response, not of the signal.

## Introduction

Skeletal muscle disposes of most postprandial glucose, and its resistance to insulin is the earliest and most consistent defect on the path to type 2 diabetes (DeFronzo and Tripathy, 2009). The molecular description of that defect has been sought mainly in two places: in insulin signalling, where impaired Akt phosphorylation is reproducible but accompanied by heterogeneous downstream effects (Tonks et al., 2013), and in the resting transcriptome, where two decades of profiling have yielded a short list of consistent findings, essentially the reduced oxidative programme marked by PGC-1α (Mootha et al., 2003; Patti et al., 2003), and many signatures that do not replicate across cohorts.

A third place has been less explored: the transcriptional response itself. Insulin regulates several hundred muscle genes within hours (Rome et al., 2003; Coletta et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2007), and specific insulin-responsive genes are blunted in obesity and diabetes (Nur77/NOR1, Fuentes et al., 2019). Insulin is also a zeitgeber: it resets the clock of adipose tissue and isolated adipocytes (Tuvia et al., 2021) and of peripheral tissues in mice (Crosby et al., 2019); the muscle clock controls insulin sensitivity (Dyar et al., 2014), circadian misalignment lowers it in humans (Wefers et al., 2018), and muscle cells from people with type 2 diabetes have intrinsically disrupted circadian oscillations (Gabriel et al., 2021). These findings have not been joined: whether insulin's reset of the clock is part of the normal muscle response in vivo, and whether it is lost in insulin resistance, is unknown. Recent proteomics has shown that the molecular signatures of insulin resistance are highly individual (Bang et al., 2024), raising the possibility that what differs between insulin-resistant people is not a shared defect but the loss of a shared response.

Here we ask a simple question with public data: is the information about insulin sensitivity in how muscle *is*, or in how it *responds*? We show that it is in the response, that the healthy response is coordinated across individuals and includes a reset of the clock output, that insulin resistance loses that coordination while keeping the magnitude, and that this loss sits within a systemic transcriptional state that the organs of a person share and that drifts continuously with disease.

## Results

### The resting muscle transcriptome does not encode insulin sensitivity

We began with the largest public dataset of clamped individuals with resting muscle biopsies (GSE182120; Gabriel et al., 2021): 24 with normal glucose tolerance and 25 with type 2 diabetes, insulin sensitivity measured by hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp (M-value 34 ± 16 vs 15 ± 9 mg/kg/min), profiled in two laboratories. After adjusting for laboratory, age and BMI, no gene among 21,595 was associated with the M-value at FDR < 0.1; 14 reached p < 0.01, fewer than the ~215 expected by chance (Figure 1A). The positive controls behaved: PPARGC1A tracked the M-value (ρ = 0.33, p = 0.02) and PDK4 was higher in diabetes (p = 0.04). Group-level geometry was equally silent: dispersion of individuals, the critical-transition index and the distance of each individual from the healthy reference did not differ between groups or track the M-value (Figure 1B). The resting state of muscle, at the resolution of bulk transcriptomics, does not know how insulin-sensitive its owner is.

### Healthy muscle responds to insulin along one shared direction, assembled over hours

We then turned to datasets in which the same individual was biopsied before and during an insulin clamp. To describe a response without choosing genes, we represented each biopsy by its position in the principal subspace of the 800 most variable genes and each individual's response as the displacement between the two biopsies. Two properties of a group's responses are then independent of magnitude: the **coherence**, the mean cosine between each individual's displacement and the leave-one-out group mean (1 when everyone moves the same way, 0 when directions are random), and the **direction** of the group mean.

In 20 insulin-sensitive individuals (GSE22309; Wu et al., 2007), coherence after 4 h of insulin was 0.77: healthy people answer insulin along a shared direction (Figure 2A). In an independent cohort of 12 healthy men (GSE9105; Coletta et al., 2008) coherence was 0.29 at 30 min and 0.77 at 4 h; after a mixed meal it was 0.51 at 1 h (GSE231509). The shared direction is not present early; it is assembled over hours (Figure 2B).

The genes that carry the shared 4-h direction, replicated across the two healthy cohorts (55 genes, same sign, |t| > 3 in both), are not the canonical insulin-signalling genes but a transcriptional programme: immediate-early and stress transcription factors (FOS, JUNB, EGR1, KLF10, FOXO1, NFE2L2), metallothioneins and HMOX1, the amino-acid transporter SLC7A5, and, most strongly, BHLHE40 (DEC1), the transcription factor through which the circadian clock couples to metabolism. In the same individuals insulin repressed the clock-output genes DBP, TEF and HLF and induced PER2 (Figure 2C): the healthy 4-h response includes a reset of the peripheral clock output, as previously shown for adipose tissue (Tuvia et al., 2021).

### Insulin-resistant muscle responds as strongly, but each individual in a different direction, and the clock does not respond

In the same cohort, 20 insulin-resistant and 15 diabetic individuals responded to insulin with the same per-individual magnitude as the sensitive group (Figure 3A). What differed was coordination: leave-one-out coherence fell to 0.35 in insulin-resistant (p = 0.013, permutation) and 0.45 in diabetic muscle (p = 0.087), and the diabetic group direction was nearly orthogonal to the healthy one (cosine 0.20 vs 0.69 under permutation, p = 0.034) (Figure 3B). Because hybridisation batch was unevenly distributed across groups in this 2007 dataset, we repeated the analysis on the 35 individuals whose two biopsies were processed in the same batch: the effect sizes were unchanged (coherence 0.76 vs 0.45 vs 0.46; cosine 0.14) and the p-values, at n ≈ 11 per group, were 0.11 and 0.15. The loss of coordination replicated in two independent arms of a trial in prediabetic women (GSE157988; Yoshino et al., 2021): coherence 0.27 and 0.43 at baseline (n = 11 and 12).

The canonical insulin programme was intact in these insulin-resistant individuals: TXNIP, KLF15 and IRS2 were repressed and PPP1R3B and HES1 induced in every subject of the prediabetic cohort (|t| > 5). What was lost was specific. Of the 55-gene healthy programme, the repressed arm (CRABP2, LTBP4, PIK3IP1, COL6A1) and part of the immediate-early arm no longer responded, and the clock coupling was lost: in a permutation test of the group-by-insulin interaction across nine clock genes, DBP (p = 0.003), PER2 (0.005) and NR1D2 (0.012) differed between sensitive and resistant individuals at FDR < 0.05, while BHLHE40 induction persisted in all groups (Figure 3C). In the same-batch subset, DBP repression was present in sensitive (−0.13 log2) and absent in resistant (−0.03) and diabetic (+0.02) muscle. The pattern converged in vitro: in myotubes from the same type of donors (GSE182117; Gabriel et al., 2021), chronic insulin repressed DBP in NGT cells (t = −3.3) more than in T2D cells (−1.7), induced BHLHE40 in both, and the amplitude of DBP, TEF, HLF and PER2 oscillations was about 30 % lower in T2D cells. Resting expression of every clock gene, in the 49 clamped biopsies of Figure 1, was identical between groups and unrelated to the M-value (all p > 0.2). The coupling between insulin and the clock is a property of the response, invisible at rest, and it is lost in insulin resistance.

### Adipose tissue fails differently: same direction, half the magnitude

Adipose tissue sampled before and during a clamp (Rydén et al., 2016; GSE26637) showed the opposite failure mode. Obese (n = 23) and insulin-resistant (n = 5) adipose tissue responded along the healthy direction (cosine 0.88–0.95) at half the magnitude (5.0 vs 7.8; 15.6 vs 28.4), with reduced coherence (0.35 vs 0.53, p = 0.07) (Figure 4A). Two years after bariatric surgery, in the same 23 women, the magnitude of the response was fully restored (7.9) while coherence was not (0.33, p = 0.55 for change); ten weeks of nicotinamide mononucleotide, which improved clamp-measured insulin sensitivity in the prediabetic women of GSE157988, did not restore coherence either (0.27 → 0.18, p = 0.57) (Figure 4B). At the sample sizes available these are absences of detectable restoration, not proof of irreversibility; they indicate that coordination of the response is a property distinct from insulin sensitivity as classically measured.

### The organs of one person share one transcriptional state that drifts continuously with disease

Finally we asked whether the response defect sits within a systemic state. We represented each tissue's coexpression network by the spectral subspace of its Laplacian and compared tissues with a cellular sheaf whose restriction maps are orthogonal alignments; the sheaf energy is a normalised Grassmann distance between tissues, zero when they share the same organisation. In 253 GTEx donors with muscle, subcutaneous adipose tissue and pancreas, the energy was 26 standard deviations below the value obtained when the gene correspondence between tissues was destroyed, after adjusting for RNA integrity, ischaemic time, batch, age, sex and cause of death (Figure 5A). The state is also the individual's: a donor's position in muscle predicted their position in adipose tissue and pancreas (canonical ρ 0.89–0.93 vs 0.23 when donors were shuffled, p = 0.002), and the same held in 19 living surgical patients across two adipose depots (0.93 vs 0.59, p = 0.0005) (Figure 5B). Whole blood from the same GTEx donors predicted 23–30 % of the principal variation of their muscle, adipose and pancreas in cross-validation.

Along the glycaemic continuum this state does not switch. In eight cohorts, three tissues and two platforms (73–434 individuals each), the distribution of individuals had one basin by sublevel-set persistence with a stability-theorem threshold; where mixture models favoured two components, they were unrelated to stage (Cramér's V 0.04–0.32) (Figure 5C). The transcriptome of each organ moves continuously, and the response defect described above is the form that movement takes in muscle.

## Discussion

Three statements summarise the work. The resting transcriptome of muscle is blind to insulin sensitivity. The healthy response to insulin is coordinated across individuals, assembled over hours, and includes a reset of the peripheral clock output. Insulin resistance preserves the magnitude of the response and the canonical signalling programme but loses the shared direction and the clock coupling.

The first statement explains an old frustration. Signatures of insulin resistance in resting muscle have rarely replicated beyond the oxidative programme; in 49 clamped individuals from two laboratories we find none at all, with the oxidative markers present as controls. If the disease is in the response, resting profiles of any size will keep finding little, and the informative design is two biopsies around a perturbation. The James laboratory reached a compatible conclusion from proteomics: the signatures of insulin resistance are personal (Bang et al., 2024). Our result gives that observation a geometry: individuals respond as strongly, but not together.

The second and third statements connect four literatures that have not cited each other. Insulin is a zeitgeber for peripheral clocks (Crosby et al., 2019; Tuvia et al., 2021); the muscle clock governs insulin sensitivity (Dyar et al., 2014); misalignment lowers insulin sensitivity in humans (Wefers et al., 2018); and the clock of diabetic muscle cells is intrinsically disrupted (Gabriel et al., 2021). We show the missing link in vivo: insulin's reset of the clock output is part of the normal 4-h response of human muscle, and it is the part that insulin-resistant muscle no longer performs, while BHLHE40, the clock's metabolic effector, still responds. DBP, TEF and HLF are direct BMAL1 targets and the PAR-bZip output through which the clock times metabolism; their failure to respond, with the clock itself unchanged at rest, locates the defect in the coupling, not in the oscillator. A response that takes hours to assemble and is clock-coupled is exactly the kind of response expected to fragment across individuals when the coupling is lost, which is what we observe.

The adipose tissue result shows that organs fail differently: muscle loses direction, adipose tissue loses magnitude. That bariatric surgery restored adipose magnitude without restoring coordination, and NMN improved sensitivity without restoring coordination, is the clinical edge of the work: coordination of the response is a measurable property that current endpoints do not capture, and a candidate endpoint for interventions that target timing (meal and insulin timing, sleep) rather than signal. Whether it is restored by anything is unknown.

The systemic state frames all of this. A person occupies one position across their metabolic organs, that position drifts continuously with disease, and there is no second basin to fall into or climb out of. This is compatible with the bistability of physiological models of diabetes (Topp et al., 2000; Ha et al., 2016) if the tissue transcriptome is a slow variable that records a fast switch without reproducing it, and it is consistent with the clinical observation that remission is maintained only while the intervention is sustained.

### Limitations of the study

The central result rests on one clamp cohort of 2007 (GSE22309, n = 20/20/15) in which hybridisation batch was unevenly distributed across groups; the same-batch analysis preserves the effect sizes at n ≈ 11 per group but not significance, and the diabetic direction in particular needs replication. Two biopsies in sequence induce an immediate-early response independent of insulin; this does not affect comparisons between groups but inflates the insulin-specificity of the healthy gene list. The prediabetic replicate is RNA-seq from one laboratory; the adipose cohorts are small. GTEx donors are post mortem, so part of the systemic state reflects terminal illness; the in vivo replicate has 19 patients. Restoration analyses are underpowered to detect partial recovery. All data are cross-sectional at the cohort level; no claim here is causal. A clamp cohort with RNA-seq at 4 h in insulin-resistant and diabetic individuals, and a paired-tissue cohort of living donors staged by glucose tolerance, would resolve what remains open; both exist (Gabriel et al., 2021; Højlund laboratory; FUSION) and we are seeking access.

Response geometry: 800 most variable genes, z-scored, principal subspace of 5 components fitted on all samples of a dataset; displacement = insulin − basal per individual; coherence = mean cosine to leave-one-out group mean; group direction = mean displacement; permutation tests by relabelling individuals; same-batch sensitivity analysis. Clock coupling: per-gene interaction (group × insulin) by permutation, Benjamini–Hochberg over nine clock genes. Resting association: Spearman of lab/age/BMI-residualised expression with M-value, BH-FDR. Cellular sheaf: biweight-midcorrelation networks (β fixed per tissue), spectral embedding of the normalised Laplacian (r = 8), Procrustes alignment to a neutral reference, Grassmann energy, gene-correspondence and donor-shuffle nulls, covariate residualisation including RIN, ischaemic time, batch, age, sex and Hardy scale.
