# A shared, individual metabolic state of the human organs loses its coordinated response to insulin along the path to type 2 diabetes

## Summary

Type 2 diabetes is studied one organ at a time, yet it is a disease of the whole organism. We asked whether the metabolic organs share a transcriptional state, whether that state is a property of the individual, and what happens to it as glucose tolerance deteriorates. Using a cellular-sheaf formulation of cross-tissue coherence and a quasi-potential landscape of patient states, we analysed 23 public transcriptomic cohorts spanning islet, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue and blood, including 253 GTEx donors with paired tissues, 19 surgical patients with paired adipose depots, and 123 individuals with muscle or adipose biopsies taken before and during hyperinsulinaemic clamps. Islet, muscle and adipose tissue share a single transcriptional architecture (z = −26 against a gene-correspondence null), and a person's position within it is the same across organs (canonical ρ = 0.93 versus 0.23 when individuals are shuffled; 0.93 versus 0.59 in vivo). Along the glycaemic continuum this state does not switch between two basins: in eight cohorts and three tissues the distribution of patients is unimodal, and the fastest transcriptional change occurs inside the prediabetic range (HbA1c 5.8–6.3 %), before diagnostic thresholds and independently of platform. What the state loses is the capacity to respond in a coordinated way: healthy muscle responds to insulin along one shared direction (coherence 0.77–0.80, two cohorts), insulin-resistant and prediabetic muscle responds idiosyncratically (0.27–0.45, three cohorts), obese and insulin-resistant adipose tissue responds along the healthy direction at half the magnitude (two cohorts), and two years after bariatric surgery the magnitude is fully restored while the coordination between individuals is not. These results define a measurable systemic state of human metabolism, place the window of fastest change before the onset of diabetes, and identify the loss of coordinated responsiveness, rather than a transition between discrete states, as the transcriptional signature of the disease.

## Introduction

The metabolic organs are usually profiled separately, and the diabetic phenotype is described as the sum of organ-specific defects: β-cell failure, muscle and liver insulin resistance, adipose inflammation. Two questions cannot be answered from that description. Whether the organs occupy a common transcriptional state, so that the state of one predicts the state of another in the same person; and whether the progression to type 2 diabetes is a transition between distinct states, as the bistable physiological models of the disease would suggest, or a continuous drift. Both questions require quantities that have not been available: a measure of coherence between organs and a measure of the geometry of the population of patient states. We introduce both, validate them on synthetic data with known structure, and apply them to the largest set of public human transcriptomes of islet, muscle, adipose tissue and blood that includes paired tissues, continuous glycaemic controls and in vivo insulin perturbations.

## Results

### 1. The metabolic organs share one transcriptional architecture, and the individual's position in it is the same across organs

We represent each tissue's coexpression network by the spectral subspace of its Laplacian and compare tissues with a cellular sheaf whose restriction maps are orthogonal alignments; its energy is a normalised Grassmann distance between tissues, zero when all organs share the same organisation. In three discovery cohorts of islet, muscle and adipose tissue from different individuals, the energy was 7–11 standard deviations below the value obtained when the gene correspondence between tissues was destroyed. In GTEx, where muscle, subcutaneous adipose tissue and pancreas come from the same 253 donors, the effect was z = −26 after adjusting for RNA integrity, ischaemic time, sequencing batch, age, sex and cause of death.

Paired tissues allow a second, stronger question: is the shared architecture also a property of the person? The first canonical correlation between a donor's scores in muscle and in adipose tissue was 0.93 (muscle–pancreas 0.91, adipose–pancreas 0.89), against 0.23 when donors were shuffled between tissues (p = 0.002). The same held in vivo: in 19 bariatric-surgery patients with subcutaneous and omental biopsies, the between-depot correlation was 0.93 against 0.59 under shuffling (p = 0.0005). An individual's deviation from the population is one deviation, expressed in every metabolic organ. Whole blood from the same GTEx donors predicted 23–30 % of the principal variation of muscle, adipose tissue and pancreas in cross-validation, indicating that part of this systemic state is visible in blood.

### 2. The state drifts continuously; there is no second basin

If health and diabetes were two attractors, the distribution of patients should be bimodal with a saddle between the basins. We estimated the quasi-potential U = −ln p on a covariate-adjusted, stage-balanced embedding and counted basins by sublevel-set persistence with a stability-theorem threshold, requiring stability across bandwidths and a mixture-model BIC in agreement. In eight cohorts, three tissues, two platforms and 73–434 individuals each, there was one basin. Where the BIC favoured two Gaussian components, the components were unrelated to glycaemic stage (Cramér's V 0.04–0.32) or consisted of a single outlier. The drift inferred by an entropic optimal-transport bridge between healthy and diabetic distributions had no detectable non-conservative component. The transcriptome of each organ moves continuously along the glycaemic axis.

### 3. The fastest change occurs in the prediabetic range

Where a continuous control was available, we estimated the Fisher–Rao information of the state distribution along it. In muscle the peak was at HbA1c 6.27 %, in islets at HbA1c 5.86 % (RNA-seq) and 5.78 % (arrays from the same 77 donors), and at fasting glucose 5.5–5.8 mmol/L in islet and adipose tissue. In every case the peak lies below the diagnostic thresholds (6.5 %, 7.0 mmol/L). The transcriptional state changes most rapidly before diabetes is diagnosed.

### 4. What is lost is the coordinated response to insulin

A hyperinsulinaemic clamp with biopsies before and during the infusion is a within-person perturbation, immune to the batch and cohort effects that limit cross-sectional comparisons. We measured, for each group, the coherence of the response direction across individuals (mean cosine to the group mean), the alignment of the group direction with the healthy one, and the net response.

In muscle, healthy individuals respond along a shared direction (coherence 0.80 at 4 h in 20 subjects; 0.77 in an independent cohort of 12). The coordination is built over time: 0.29 at 30 min, 0.51 at 1 h after a mixed meal, 0.77–0.80 at 4 h. Insulin-resistant (0.45, n = 20) and prediabetic muscle (0.43 and 0.27, two arms of a trial, n = 23) respond with the same magnitude as healthy muscle but along idiosyncratic directions, so that the net group response halves. Diabetic muscle (n = 15) responded along a direction nearly orthogonal to the healthy one (cosine 0.25; net response −50 %, p = 0.03); this observation comes from a single cohort and a 1-h mixed-meal study at n = 7 did not reproduce it.

In adipose tissue the failure mode is different. Obese (n = 23) and insulin-resistant (n = 5) adipose tissue responds along the healthy direction (cosine 0.88–0.95) but at half the magnitude, with reduced coherence (0.35 versus 0.53, p = 0.07). Two years after bariatric surgery in the same 23 women, the magnitude of the response was fully restored (7.9 versus 7.8 in non-obese controls) while the coherence between individuals was not (0.33). Ten weeks of nicotinamide mononucleotide, which improved clamp-measured insulin sensitivity in prediabetic women, did not restore coordination in muscle either (0.27 to 0.18). Coordination of the response is therefore a property distinct from insulin sensitivity as classically measured, and it is the property that neither intervention recovered.

## Discussion

Three things follow. First, there is a systemic transcriptional state of human metabolism: the organs share one architecture and the person occupies one position in it. The construction that makes this measurable, a sheaf over the tissue graph, is general and applies to any set of paired tissues. Second, the progression to type 2 diabetes does not pass through a second basin. This is compatible with the bistability of the physiological models if the tissue transcriptome is a slow variable that records a fast switch in glucose–β-cell dynamics without reproducing it; it predicts, and the clinical literature confirms, that transcriptionally measured remission is sustained only while the control (weight, glycaemia) is held. Third, the fastest change precedes diagnosis, and what changes is the capacity to respond in a coordinated way. Healthy organs respond to insulin together, along one direction that takes hours to assemble; insulin-resistant organs respond as strongly but each in its own direction, and that coordination is not restored by interventions that restore magnitude or sensitivity.

The clinical reading is that the prediabetic window, not the diagnosis, is where the transcriptome moves, and that the relevant endpoint of an intervention is whether it re-coordinates the response, not only whether it lowers glucose. The blood readout of the systemic state (R² 0.2–0.3 in paired donors) is the path to measuring this without biopsies; in the small blood cohorts available it did not separate prediabetes from health, so this remains a prospect.

Limitations are explicit. The cross-sectional cohorts are different individuals per tissue except in GTEx (post mortem) and one surgical cohort; the orthogonal diabetic response in muscle rests on one cohort; group sizes in the clamp studies are 5–23; and the disease label is unavailable for GTEx. The claim that the state contracts along the progression, present in the discovery cohorts, did not replicate and is not made. A paired-tissue cohort of living donors with glycaemic staging would resolve what remains open.
